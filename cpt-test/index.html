The soil profile beneath a site off Highland Road often bears zero resemblance to what you find three miles south near Gardere. That is the reality in Baton Rouge, where the Mississippi River has spent millennia laying down point bars, abandoned channels, and thick cohesive clays in chaotic sequence. A standard split-spoon sample every five feet simply misses the interbedded silt seams that govern drainage and settlement. We run the CPT test to capture a continuous resistivity and pore pressure signature through these coastal plain deposits. Our 20-ton penetrometer truck navigates tight urban lots and industrial yards alike, pushing through soft fat clays and dense sand lenses to refusal. This approach quantifies tip resistance and sleeve friction directly, without sample disturbance, which is critical when designing shallow mat foundations on the compressible alluvium common across East Baton Rouge Parish.

A single CPT sounding in Baton Rouge alluvium replaces a dozen split-spoon samples and delivers a continuous pore pressure profile that SPT simply cannot provide.

How we work

A recent project near the ExxonMobil refinery complex required bearing capacity verification beneath a proposed tank farm. The upper 12 feet were soft, normally consolidated clays deposited by the ancestral Mississippi; below that, we encountered a dense Pleistocene sand unit that varied in elevation by almost eight feet across a 200-foot grid. The CPT test mapped that interface with centimeter-scale accuracy using cone tip resistance and friction ratio, flagging zones where differential settlement could crack a ring wall. We pushed to 80 feet at multiple locations, measuring equilibrium pore pressure dissipation to confirm the sand was freely draining and not a perched lens. For projects in Baton Rouge where lateral spreading is a concern during hurricane storm surge events, this data pairs naturally with our liquefaction analysis that applies Seed-Idriss simplified procedures to the corrected cone data. The continuous profile eliminates the blind spots that plague SPT-based investigations in interlayered deltaic sequences.

Site-specific factors

The rig itself is a 20-ton CPT truck with a continuous hydraulic push system and an integrated cone that measures three channels simultaneously: tip resistance (qc), sleeve friction (fs), and dynamic pore pressure (u2). Skipping a CPT test in Baton Rouge means relying on disturbed samples and SPT blow counts alone, which notoriously underestimate the sensitivity of Pleistocene-age clays when remolded. We have seen projects where a 15-foot-thick soft clay lens was missed entirely by borings spaced 50 feet apart; the subsequent fill placement triggered a bearing failure that delayed construction by eight weeks. East Baton Rouge Parish sits on the Baton Rouge fault system, a series of east-west trending growth faults. The continuous qc profile from a CPT test reveals offset strata that hint at fault-related subsidence, a risk that no discrete sampling program can adequately characterize before foundation design is locked in.

Other technical services

01

Piezocone (CPTu) Profiling for Foundation Design

We push a piezocone to refusal or target depth, recording qc, fs, and u2 simultaneously. Data is processed to estimate undrained shear strength in clays, relative density in sands, and soil behavior type classification using normalized charts. The continuous pore pressure record identifies drainage boundaries that govern consolidation settlement calculations.

02

Seismic CPT for Shear Wave Velocity

We deploy a seismic cone with a triaxial geophone at 1-meter intervals to measure downhole shear wave velocity (Vs). The resulting Vs profile feeds site class determination per ASCE 7-22 and enables liquefaction triggering analysis without relying on empirical SPT-Vs correlations that lose accuracy in the interbedded silts common beneath Baton Rouge.

Quick answers

How deep can a CPT test penetrate in Baton Rouge soils?

Our 20-ton rig typically reaches between 80 and 100 feet in the alluvial and Pleistocene deposits around Baton Rouge. Depth is limited by cone refusal on dense gravel stringers or the Pleistocene basal gravel that underlies much of the parish. We monitor sleeve friction and inclination in real time to abort before damaging the cone string.

What does a CPT test in Baton Rouge cost per sounding?

For projects within East Baton Rouge Parish, a standard piezocone sounding to refusal generally ranges from US$180 to US$240 per vertical meter, depending on the number of soundings, site access conditions, and whether dissipation tests or seismic modules are required. Mobilization is quoted separately based on distance from our staging yard.

Can CPT replace all soil borings on a Baton Rouge project?

CPT provides continuous stratigraphic data without physical samples, but most regulatory agencies in Louisiana still require a minimum number of borings with split-spoon sampling for index testing. We typically recommend a hybrid program: CPT soundings at closer spacing to map stratigraphic continuity, interspersed with fewer boreholes to recover samples for lab classification and to confirm soil behavior type interpretations.

How do you correct CPT data for the soft clays common near the Mississippi River?

Our data processing applies pore pressure corrections to the cone tip resistance (qt = qc + u2(1-a)) per ASTM D5778, which is essential in the normally consolidated clays of the river floodplain where excess pore pressure can exceed 50% of the measured tip resistance. We also apply overburden stress normalization to friction ratio for soil behavior type classification, using iterative procedures that account for the low effective stresses near the surface.

Do you perform dissipation tests during a CPT sounding?

Yes, pore pressure dissipation tests are standard in our CPTu program for Baton Rouge sites. We stop the push at predetermined depths—typically within identified sand layers—and monitor u2 decay over time until 50% consolidation (t50) is reached. The dissipation curves yield in-situ horizontal coefficient of consolidation, a parameter that no laboratory test on a disturbed sample can reliably reproduce.
