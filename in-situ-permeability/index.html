The Mississippi River floodplain defines subsurface conditions across Baton Rouge, where alluvial silts and clays from the Holocene epoch alternate with Pleistocene terrace deposits. At elevations barely above 56 feet, the water table here sits unusually high, often within 5 to 10 feet of the surface in lower-lying wards toward the river. That shallow groundwater, combined with the lenticular nature of point-bar sands embedded in the clay matrix, creates a permeability profile that is anything but uniform. A test pit investigation can visually confirm the layering of these overbank deposits, but quantifying the actual hydraulic conductivity demands direct measurement. In our experience reviewing projects from the LSU lakes area to industrial sites near Port Allen, assuming a textbook permeability value for 'silty clay' has led more than one dewatering plan to fail during excavation. The Lefranc and Lugeon field permeability test methods, performed downhole, provide the site-specific K-value that geotechnical and environmental engineers need to model seepage with confidence.

In Baton Rouge's backswamp deposits, a single sand seam can carry more water than the entire clay column above it — isolating that seam during a Lefranc test changes the dewatering design completely.

How we work

We mobilize a modular test setup built around a twin-packer assembly for Lugeon testing in fractured or cemented Pleistocene strata, and a simple open-borehole or screened-casing configuration for Lefranc falling- and rising-head tests in the softer alluvium. The equipment is compact enough to operate inside a hollow-stem auger or directly in a cased borehole, with a submersible pressure transducer logging water levels at one-second intervals. What sets the approach apart for Baton Rouge projects is the real-time compensation for barometric swings caused by the Gulf moisture influence; our transducers are vented to atmosphere and temperature-compensated, which eliminates the drift that cheaper unvented sensors introduce. Where the boring log shows a sand seam at 22 feet, the Lefranc test isolates that zone to determine if it communicates with the river or acts as a confined lens. This same principle applies when we combine the data with a cone penetration test profile, using the CPT's sleeve friction and pore pressure dissipation curves to identify the most permeable horizons before setting the packer.

Site-specific factors

The contrast between the Garden District, sitting on the higher Prairie Terrace with oxidized clays, and the low-lying wetlands near Bayou Manchac illustrates the risk of skipping field permeability testing. On the terrace, Lugeon tests often reveal fractured silty clay with secondary permeability along desiccation cracks, meaning water moves through the formation far faster than a lab permeameter would suggest. Down by the bayou, where organic silts and peats dominate, Lefranc tests consistently show K-values two orders of magnitude lower, but the material is so compressible that any unplanned seepage triggers settlement. We have seen projects along Highland Road where groundwater inflow into a deep excavation, unaccounted for because the geotech relied on grain-size correlations alone, added weeks to the schedule. The IBC Chapter 18 requires groundwater control measures to be based on actual site characterization, and the field permeability test is the direct measurement that satisfies that requirement when dealing with layered alluvial sequences.

Other technical services

01

Lefranc Rising & Falling Head Testing

Performed in soil borings to isolate specific alluvial horizons. We use a screened casing with a sand pack and a bentonite seal above the test zone. Data loggers capture the head recovery curve, and we calculate K using Hvorslev's time-lag method. Ideal for dewatering system design in point-bar sand lenses.

02

Lugeon Packer Testing in Rock

For the deeper Pleistocene gravels and the underlying clay-shale, we deploy a pneumatic or mechanical packer system. A constant-pressure injection at up to 10 bar measures the rock mass permeability. We plot Lugeon units against pressure steps to identify dilation or hydraulic fracturing behavior.

03

Permeability Profile Integration

We synthesize the K-value log with the CPT pore pressure dissipation data, Atterberg limits, and grain-size curves from the same boring. The deliverable is a composite geohydrologic profile that maps the hydraulic conductivity with depth, directly supporting MODFLOW or SEEP/W model calibration.

Quick answers

What is the typical cost of a field permeability test in Baton Rouge?

For a single Lefranc test in a soil boring, the cost ranges from US$590 to US$1,020 depending on depth and test zone length. A full Lugeon profile with multiple pressure steps in rock typically adds to the upper end of that range, as it requires additional packer setup time and pressure transducer calibration.

How long does a Lefranc test take to complete?

A single falling-head test in a silty sand seam might stabilize within 15 to 30 minutes; in low-permeability clay, the test can run for an hour or more. We maintain the test until the head change rate drops below 0.01 feet per minute over three consecutive readings to ensure a valid equilibrium analysis.

Which test is better for Baton Rouge's soils, Lefranc or Lugeon?

It depends on the formation. In the soft alluvial silts and sands above 40 feet, the Lefranc method is the standard because it does not fracture the soil. When we encounter the Pleistocene terrace gravels or the stiff clay-shale beneath them, we switch to the Lugeon test to evaluate secondary permeability through fissures and joints under controlled pressure.

Can you test permeability in an existing monitoring well?

Yes, provided the well has a properly installed sand pack and the screen is not clogged. We run a rising-head Lefranc test by introducing a slug of water and logging the recovery. Keep in mind that the result reflects the average permeability of the entire screened interval, not a discrete layer, so it works well for aquifer characterization but less so for a precise excavation dewatering model.
