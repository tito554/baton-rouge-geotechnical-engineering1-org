Baton Rouge sits on the Pleistocene terrace deposits of the Mississippi River, where fat clays and silty soils dominate the subsurface. The water table often sits just 5 to 10 feet below grade, keeping natural moisture content high year-round. That combination creates real challenges for pavement engineers because soaked subgrades lose bearing capacity fast. The laboratory CBR test gives us a direct measurement of that resistance under controlled moisture and density conditions, per ASTM D1883. For projects along I-10 or new subdivisions in the Livingston Parish side of the metro, we pair the soaked CBR value with field density from the sand cone density test to confirm compaction targets are actually achieved in the fill. Many LADOTD jobs also require correlating the lab CBR with the CBR road test for in-situ verification before placing base course.

A 96-hour soaked CBR on Baton Rouge fat clay often drops below 3%, which demands a thicker aggregate base or lime stabilization to meet the design structural number.

How we work

With over 220,000 residents and major infrastructure tied to the Port of Greater Baton Rouge, pavement performance is not optional—it is a daily operational requirement. The standard lab procedure follows ASTM D1883, where a 6-inch diameter remolded specimen is compacted at optimum moisture and soaked for 96 hours to simulate worst-case field conditions. A 3-square-inch piston penetrates at 0.05 inches per minute while we record load at 0.1-inch and 0.2-inch penetration. For heavy industrial yards near the ExxonMobil refinery complex, we often run the test at modified Proctor energy to match the compaction spec. The raw load-penetration curve tells you more than a single number—it reveals whether the material will punch-shear or densify under repeated axle loads. This data feeds directly into flexible pavement thickness design using the AASHTO 1993 method, which LADOTD still references for many secondary road projects.

Site-specific factors

The loading press applies a constant strain rate through a mechanical or hydraulic jack, with a calibrated 10,000-lbf ring or electronic load cell reading penetration resistance. In Baton Rouge, the main failure mode we catch is a soaked CBR below the 6% minimum that LADOTD typically requires for subgrade—values of 2-3% are common in untreated CH clays from the Port Hudson formation. Running the test at field moisture without proper soaking misses the point entirely. A pavement designed on an optimistic, unsoaked CBR fails early: rutting appears after the first wet season, edge cracking follows, and the asphalt mat disintegrates within five years. We report the corrected CBR at 0.1-inch and 0.2-inch penetration, flagging any swell exceeding 0.5% during soaking. When the subgrade shows borderline values, we recommend lime stabilization and retest the treated mix to confirm the improvement before the structural section is finalized.

Quick answers

What soaked CBR value does LADOTD require for subgrade in Baton Rouge?

LADOTD typically requires a minimum soaked CBR of 6% at 95% Standard Proctor density for subgrade. In areas with highly plastic clays—common across East Baton Rouge Parish—values can drop to 2-3% without treatment, triggering a requirement for lime stabilization or a thicker aggregate base. The specification is detailed in the LADOTD Standard Specifications, Sections 302-305.

How much does a laboratory CBR test cost in Baton Rouge?

A single-point soaked CBR test on a remolded specimen typically runs between US$120 and US$200, depending on whether Proctor compaction data is already available and if the test is part of a larger geotechnical package. Multi-point CBR curves for lime-treated mixes fall at the higher end of that range.

Do you test both standard and modified Proctor energy for CBR?

Yes. We run CBR at both Standard Proctor (ASTM D698) and Modified Proctor (ASTM D1557) depending on the design traffic. Modified effort is common for industrial pavements and port facilities around Baton Rouge where heavy forklifts and container handlers generate higher contact pressures.

Can the lab CBR be used directly in AASHTO pavement design?

Absolutely. The soaked CBR is the primary input for the structural number calculation in the AASHTO 1993 flexible pavement design method, which remains the basis for LADOTD secondary road design. We report the corrected CBR at 0.1-inch penetration—the value that goes into the design equation—along with swell data and compaction details.
